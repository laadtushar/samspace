One line

The two you chose, and five drawn to sit beside them. Each is shown large, then at the sizes a favicon is actually used at, with its separately drawn reduction.

What makes these a family

  • One path, one M, no Z — genuinely one unbroken line, never closed. Checked against every file, not taken on trust.
  • A coil that tightens, or a deliberate refusal of one. It is the strongest gesture the two you picked have in common.
  • The line does two jobs — in K the contour becomes the interior; in J the tangle and the spiral are the same thread.
  • Emptiness carries the mark. Most of each one is bare ground.
  • One stroke, so one colour. None of the five took clay: a continuous line cannot change colour partway without becoming two drawings. Warmth here would have to come from the ground, not the line.

J — The Thread your pick

A loose tangle lets one thread go; it crosses a wide empty middle and winds into a close, still spiral. Nothing is cut and nothing added — the same length of thread, wound.

K — Profile your pick

Throat, chin, lip, nose, brow, forehead, crown, down behind the ear — then it hooks in at the nape and coils twice inside the head. Open at the throat; it never closes.

JK5 — The Long Question

It begins as a ruled line — the line you write on — and runs level and almost empty across three quarters of the mark; then it lifts off the rule into the hook of a question. Where a question mark puts a separate dot, this line does not lift: it carries on down and winds into the coil.

Its pick. The only one of the five that survives 16px with its idea intact, the only one that speaks to the academic half of the practice, and its construction move — the dot that is not a dot, because the line never lifted — is exactly K's move transposed. It also gives you an armature nothing else does: a rule a wordmark can sit on. Caveat it raised itself: the most literal device in the set, and the one that risks reading as stock punctuation.

JK1 — Between Two

Two people facing each other, drawn without lifting. The line runs down the first face to the throat, carries on across the gap and rises as the second figure's throat — so the second person is the first person's own line continued. Then it leaves both bodies, crosses a bare field, and winds into a spiral belonging to neither.

The best mark at full size, and its icon cannot carry the idea. It tried five icon layouts; in every one the two noses merge and the pair reads as a single animal head with ears. The shipped icon drops the first figure entirely — which costs the direction its whole point at favicon size and leaves the silhouette close to K's. A fair trade if the practice is mostly seen at header size; not if the favicon matters.

JK2 — The Unwinding

The family gesture run backwards. It starts at the dead centre of a tight coil and every turn is wider than the last, so the spiral accelerates instead of settling; the final turn does not close, it leaves, and the thread runs away and simply stops on a bare open end.

Best-behaved of the five at 32px. Two caveats, both its own: it sits close to M-loose-end, which is also “released rather than wound”; and read cold it can look like a decorative flourish, because we read left to right — tail into centre — which is winding.

JK3 — Not Yet Pulled

A thread comes in bare, crosses itself three times — an overhand knot, drawn open, never pulled — and runs straight out the other side into the coil. The crossings are the subject: not a tangle, three decisions. It can still be undone from either end.

The most useful failure in the round. Three crossings at 32px with a heavy stroke is not achievable while keeping the tails: open it enough and two crossings vanish and it reads as a fish; keep it dense and the lobes fill solid. The shipped icon gives up the spiral and the runs and keeps only the knot — which holds all three crossings, and reads as a heraldic trefoil. The idea and the icon pull in opposite directions.

JK4 — Through

The line runs in level, dips, and winds inward to a tight centre — then, without reversing and without lifting, keeps turning the same way while the radius grows, comes back out between its own turns, crosses its own rim and goes on, a little lower than it came in.

Its own drop, and the most original idea of the five. The coil is in the middle of the line, not at its end: the tight place is somewhere the thread went through, not somewhere it stayed. Dropped because beside JK2 it is the same picture at a glance. Its first version read unmistakably as a breaking wave until the tangents were solved so the thread enters and leaves level.

Seen stacked, four of the five sit on the same armature — a long horizontal run with an event on it — which is J's armature, so it is legitimately in-family, but it makes the sheet more repetitive than J and K alone. JK1 is the only one with a different silhouette. If you want five that sit apart from each other as well as beside J and K, one of JK2, JK3 or JK4 should be replaced with something vertical or square.
